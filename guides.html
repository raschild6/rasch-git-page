Action Scheduler Experimental

Let Rasch-Git fetch, pull, push or run maintenance for you on a schedule — across all your repositories, a tab group, or just the ones you pick.

Experimental. Scheduled actions run without asking, and pull, stash + pull + unstash and push change working trees (push also changes the remote). Start with fetch-only rules, review every rule, and turn on anything that changes files only once you trust it.

What it does

A rule is an action, some repositories and a trigger: what to run, where and when. Rules run while the app is open, in the background:

  • Scheduled work waits for your own operations — it never runs over something you are doing.
  • Every change a scheduled action makes can be undone, like your own.
  • Successful runs show one short summary; failures and conflicts are collected in one Problems window instead of interrupting you.
  • Missed runs (laptop asleep, app closed) are caught up once, not in a burst.
  • The status bar always shows the next run, e.g. Next: Fetch everything · tomorrow 08:00.

Create your first rule

  1. Open Edit → Scheduled Actions… and stay on the Rules tab.
  2. Click New and give the rule a name you will recognize in the status bar and in the Problems window — e.g. Fetch everything.
  3. Choose the action (see the table below). For a first rule, pick Fetch: it never touches your working tree.
  4. Under Repositories, choose On: all open repositories, a tab group, or chosen repositories. When you pick a group, the dialog shows how many repositories that means right now (e.g. 3 open repositories now).
  5. For actions that work on branches, choose Branches (see stash + pull + unstash).
  6. Under When, set the trigger, then click Save rule. The tick in the first column enables or disables a rule without deleting it.
  7. Click Run now once to check the rule does what you expect before waiting for its first scheduled run.
Duplicate is the fastest way to make a variant of a rule — e.g. the same pull, but for a different group and time.

Active window: interval rules that respect your day

An interval rule can be limited to an active window, so “every hour” means every hour during the day, not all night. The rule list shows it next to the interval, as in Every 1 h, 08:00–19:00.

ExampleKeep everything fetched during office hours
  1. Action: Fetch · Repositories: All open repositories.
  2. When: every 60 minutes, only between 08:00 and 19:00.
  3. Save. Your branch labels and ahead / behind counts stay current all day, and nothing in your working trees changes.

Stash + pull + unstash, step by step

This is the “start the day up to date” action. For each repository in the rule:

  1. The checked-out branch: your local changes are stashed, the branch is pulled, and the changes are restored.
  2. Other chosen branches are fast-forwarded without checking them out, so your working tree is not switched.
  3. Branches: pick Current branch (the one checked out in each repository) or Chosen branches — names or patterns such as main, develop or release/*. A repository that has none of them is skipped.
ExampleMorning update for one project
  1. Action: Stash + Pull + Unstash · Repositories: A tab group → Orbit.
  2. Branches: Chosen branches → tick main and develop.
  3. When: weekdays at 09:00.
  4. Result: at nine, main and develop are current in every Orbit repository, and whatever you had in progress is back in place.

When something goes wrong

Failures never pop up in the middle of your work. They go to the Problems window, and the status bar turns red with a count (e.g. 2 problems). Other repositories and rules keep running.

  • Repeated failures are grouped: the same error from the same rule shows once with a counter (×3 · last 30/09 22:57).
  • Authentication failed: use Fix sign-in… (see Accounts & SSH keys), then Retry.
  • Conflict: the repository is paused — the scheduler leaves it alone until you resolve the conflict, while the others carry on. Your local changes are kept in the stash, named after the rule, e.g. “Scheduled update Morning update”. Click Go to repository to resolve it.
  • Acknowledge an entry once you have dealt with it; Acknowledge all, Clear acknowledged and Show acknowledged keep the list tidy.
  • In Scheduled Actions…, Pause scheduler stops future runs and Stop running jobs stops the ones in progress. The History tab shows what ran.

Before you schedule: short intervals and pushes

Short intervals on many repositories load your machine and the Git server, and actions repeated in quick succession can pile up. Prefer hourly fetches over “every minute”, and use an active window.
Scheduling a push: push rules must be confirmed, never force-push and skip protected branches — but a push still publishes whatever is committed at that moment, including commits you made a minute ago and did not mean to share yet, and undo does not revert pushes. If you schedule pushes at all, limit them to chosen branches and one group, and watch the rule with Run now first.
A safe default setup: Fetch for everything during the day, plus one Stash + Pull + Unstash in the morning for the project you work on. Leave push and pull on many repositories to Manage Repos, where you see the result immediately.

Bulk Repo & Branch Management

Clean up dozens of branches or update every open repository in one action — with a report of what happened to each one.

Manage Branches: many branches of one repository

Open Edit → Manage Branches…. You get every branch of the current repository, with whether it exists locally, on the remote, and its upstream. The current branch is marked with ★.

  1. Type in Filter branches… to narrow the list — e.g. feature/.
  2. Tick the branches (or Select all).
  3. Pick an Action: delete (locally, on the remote, or both), pull, or merge.
  4. Click Run. When it finishes you get a summary report.
ExampleDelete merged feature branches — safely
  1. Filter: feature/ → Select all.
  2. Action: Delete local. Leave Force delete (unmerged) unchecked.
  3. Run. Merged branches are deleted; branches with unmerged work are refused, not lost. The current branch is never deleted.
Changed your mind? Bulk branch actions can be undone with Edit → Undo (see Undo / Redo).
ExampleBring every local branch up to date
  1. Select all → Action: Pull → Run.
  2. Branches that can fast-forward are updated without being checked out; the rest are reported.

Reading the summary report

ResultMeaningWhat to do
✓ OKDone — e.g. “Fast-forwarded 2 commits” or “Already up to date”.Nothing.
⚠ Conflicte.g. “not a fast-forward — requires manual resolution”: the branch and its upstream have diverged.Check the branch out and pull or rebase it yourself.
– Skippede.g. “No upstream branch”: there was nothing to do.Set an upstream if you expected it to be updated.

The work continues past individual failures, so one bad branch never stops the batch. Copy report puts the whole table on the clipboard — handy for a stand-up note or a ticket.

Manage Repos: one action across all open repositories

Open Edit → Manage Repos…. It lists every open repository with its path and current branch. Tick the ones you want (or Select all), pick an action — fetch, pull, checkout, create branch, push or delete branch — and Run.

ExampleStart a release branch in every service
  1. Select all → Action: Create branch.
  2. Branch: release/1.4.0 · keep Checkout after create ticked.
  3. Run, then run Push on the same selection to publish the branch.
ExampleRemove a finished branch everywhere, remote included
  1. Select all → Action: Delete branch · Branch: feature/search-ui.
  2. Tick Also delete on remote. Leave Force (unmerged) unchecked so unmerged copies are refused.
  3. Run and read the summary report for any repository where the branch could not be deleted.
Organize related repositories into a tab group first; it makes it easy to see which repositories a bulk action is about to touch.

Custom Toolbar Buttons

Turn the commands you type every day into one-click toolbar buttons that know which repository, branch and remote you are on.

Start from the six default buttons

Rasch-Git comes with six ready-made buttons, each with the right command for macOS, Windows and Linux. If you don’t see them, open Edit → Custom Toolbar Buttons… and click Add default buttons.

ButtonWhat it does
Copy Remote URLCopies the repository’s remote URL to the clipboard.
Copy Branch NameCopies the current branch name to the clipboard.
Open in FolderOpens the repository folder in Explorer, Finder or your file manager.
Open in TerminalOpens a system terminal in the repository folder.
Open in VS CodeOpens the repository in Visual Studio Code.
Open on GitHubOpens the repository’s page on GitHub in your browser.

The default buttons are shell commands (on Linux the two copy buttons and Open in Terminal use the app’s own actions instead, so they work without extra tools), so they are also the best example of how to write your own. Select one to see its command — Copy Remote URL on macOS, for instance, is:

printf %s "{remote_url}" | pbcopy
Each button can show its icon with your label, or only the icon — give each button an icon you can recognize. When there are more buttons than fit, the toolbar scrolls sideways.

Create or edit a button

  1. Open Edit → Custom Toolbar Buttons….
  2. Click Add — or select a default button and click Duplicate to start from a working command.
  3. Fill in the Label (the text under the icon) and pick an Icon.
  4. Choose the Action: a Git action, an app action, a URL or text template, or a shell command. Shell commands run in the repository folder, e.g. make test.
  5. Use ▲ / ▼ to order the buttons, then OK. Untick Show the built-in Pull / Push / Stash buttons if you want only your own.

Placeholders are filled in from the active repository when you click the button:

PlaceholderBecomes
{repo_path}The repository folder
{repo_name}The repository name — the last part of its folder path
{branch}The current branch
{commit}The current commit hash
{short_commit}The short commit hash
{remote_url}The remote URL
{remote_owner}The owner (user or organization) part of the remote
{remote_repo}The repository part of the remote
Placeholder values are escaped before the command runs, so a branch with a strange name can’t inject commands.

Examples: variations on the defaults

ExampleCopy a clone command instead of the bare URL

Duplicate Copy Remote URL and change the command (macOS shown):

printf %s "git clone {remote_url}" | pbcopy

Paste it straight into a chat when someone asks “how do I get this repo?”.

ExampleCopy a branch link for a review request

Duplicate Copy Branch Name and turn it into a link to the branch on GitHub (macOS shown):

printf %s "https://github.com/{remote_owner}/{remote_repo}/tree/{branch}" | pbcopy
ExampleOpen the CI/CD page for the current branch

Start from Open on GitHub, or make a URL button. For GitHub Actions:

https://github.com/{remote_owner}/{remote_repo}/actions?query=branch%3A{branch}

For GitLab pipelines:

https://gitlab.com/{remote_owner}/{remote_repo}/-/pipelines?ref={branch}
ExampleOpen in a different editor

Duplicate Open in VS Code, rename it, and replace the editor command with yours — for example:

subl .

for Sublime Text, or idea . for IntelliJ IDEA. Commands run in the repository folder, so . is the repository.

Examples: your own commands

ExampleRun the tests

Action: Shell Command:

npm test

or pytest -q, make check… Shell commands run through the same runner as Git: you can cancel them, and they are recorded in the logs.

ExampleBuild and tag a Docker image

Action: Shell Command:

docker build -t {repo_name}:{short_commit} .
ExampleYour own Pull / Stash

Action: a Git action such as pull, push, stash, merge or rebase — useful if you hide the built-in buttons and want a different set.

A shell-command button runs with your user’s permissions. Only add commands you understand, and never paste one from an untrusted source.
Buttons can also be run by the Action Scheduler — e.g. a nightly “Run tests” on a tab group.

Deep Search

Find any file by name — even one that was renamed or deleted long ago — or any line of code, in every branch and across the whole history.

The window has three searches, chosen with the buttons at the top (it reopens on the one you used last): File names, Text in branches and Text in history. This guide starts with file names; for code, jump to Find where code lives, and when it changed.

Run a search

  1. Open Edit → Deep Search… or press Ctrl+Shift+F, and choose File names.
  2. File name: type a name, part of a name, or a glob such as *.sql or src/**/test_*.py.
  3. Max commits (optional): leave All history, or limit how many commits to search in a very large repository.
  4. Date range (optional): tick From and / or To and pick the dates.
  5. Click Search. It runs in the background with a progress bar, and you can cancel it.

Reading the results

Every row is one commit that touched a matching file: Commit, Date, Author, Message, the Change (added, modified, renamed or deleted) and the File path. The line above the table sums it up, e.g. 6 match(es) in 196 commit(s).

Click a row and the graph jumps to that commit — loading older history if needed — while the diff viewer shows that file's changes.

Examples

Example“When was the old config deleted?”
  1. File name: legacy.yml → Search.
  2. Look for the row whose Change is deleted; the message and author tell you why and who.
  3. Click the last modified row before the deletion to see the file’s final contents.
Example“Where did everything about payments come from?”
  1. File name: payments. A fragment is enough — it matches src/orbit/services/payments.py and tests/test_payments.py.
  2. The oldest rows (added) show when each file was created; the rest are its history.
Example“Which SQL migrations were added this year?”
  1. File name: *.sql · tick From and set it to 1 January.
  2. Focus on the rows whose Change is added.
To find a commit by its message, author or hash, use commit search in the graph (Ctrl+F) instead.

Find where code lives, and when it changed

Two text searches answer two different questions:

SearchAnswersLooks at
Text in branches“Where is parse_config still used?”The files as they are now, at the tip of your branches
Text in history“When was OLD_API_KEY added, and when was it removed?”The changes made by each commit, even in files deleted long ago

Text in branches

  1. Choose Text in branches and type the text in Text.
  2. Branches: all branches (local and remote), local branches, or the current branch.
  3. Optionally narrow it with In files — e.g. src/**/*.py, *.md — and tick Match case, Whole word or Regex.
  4. Search. Results are grouped by file, with the line number, the match highlighted and the branches that have that line (main +3): the same line on many branches is one row.
  5. Double-click a line to open the blame of that file, at that branch, on that line.

Text in history

  1. Choose Text in history and type the text.
  2. Pick what to look for:
    • When was it added or removed? (the default) finds the commits that changed how many times the text appears — the commit that introduced it, and the one that removed it. Code that was only moved around isn't reported.
    • Any changed line that matches finds every commit with an added or removed line matching the text — use it with Regex to follow a pattern through history.
  3. Max commits limits how far back it looks (5000 by default; 0 searches everything).
  4. Each result says whether the text was added or removed, with the matching line. Click it to see the commit and the file's diff.
Example“Did that old key ever get committed?”
  1. Text in history · Text: OLD_API_KEY · Max commits: 0.
  2. The added row shows who introduced it and when; the removed row shows when it went away. If it was ever committed, it's still in the history — rotate it.
Example“Which branches still call the old function?”
  1. Text in branches · Text: legacy_login( · Branches: all branches.
  2. Each row lists the branches that still have that line — merge or clean them up before deleting the function.

Regex tips

  • def +parse_[a-z_]+ — every function whose name starts with parse_.
  • TODO|FIXME — either word.
  • ^import — only lines that start with import.
  • Without Regex, the text is matched literally — handy for code full of brackets and dots.

Right-click any result for File History, Blame or Show in Graph. Every search runs in the background, shows results as they arrive and can be cancelled.

Text searches look at committed content only — not uncommitted changes, untracked files, stashes or tags — and skip binary files. Results stop at 2000 matching lines; refine the search if you hit the limit.

External Editors

Open images, PDFs, spreadsheets and anything else in the program you choose — the current file or any committed version of it.

Where files open externally

  • Right-click a file in the changes panel or in a commit's file list → Open with System Default or Open with….
  • Double-click a PDF, office document or other binary file — it opens in its program instead of an empty diff. Images open in the app's image diff instead; its header has Open externally for the old and new versions, and with View → Image Diff off they open in their program.
  • Open Externally in the diff viewer's header.

Map extensions to programs

  1. Open Edit → External Editors….
  2. Click Add and fill in a Label (e.g. Spreadsheets) and the Extensions, comma-separated with the dot: .csv, .xlsx.
  3. Set the Program with Browse…, or tick Use the system default application.
  4. Order the rows with Move Up / Move Down, then OK. Restore Defaults brings back the original list.

How the right program is chosen

  1. The rows are checked from top to bottom, and the first row whose extensions match the file wins.
  2. If no row matches, the file opens with the operating system’s default application.
ExampleOrder matters

You have Images → Photoshop for .png, .jpg, .psd and, further down, Screenshots → Preview for .png. A .png always opens in Photoshop, because that row comes first. Move Screenshots above Images and .png files open in Preview while .jpg and .psd still go to Photoshop.

Useful mappings

LabelExtensionsProgram
Spreadsheets.csv, .xlsxExcel, LibreOffice Calc or Numbers
Images.png, .jpg, .svgPreview, Photoshop, GIMP…
Markdown.mdYour editor, e.g. VS Code or Typora
Documents.pdfTick Use the system default application
Opening a file from a past commit or a stash opens a temporary copy — it is cleaned up when the app closes, and edits to it do not change your repository. To change the file, open the version in your working tree.

Clean Up Your Branch Before a Pull Request

Turn “wip”, “fix typo” and “oops” into a short, readable series of commits with the visual interactive rebase — and undo the whole thing in one step if you change your mind.

Open the editor

  1. Check out the branch you want to clean up. Interactive rebase works on the checked-out branch.
  2. In the graph, right-click the oldest commit you want to change → Edit History. (Or right-click the current branch's label → Interactive Rebase….)
  3. The dialog lists that commit and every commit after it, newest on top. Select a row to see its changes below the list.

Choose what happens to each commit

ActionKeyWhat it does
PickPKeep the commit as it is.
RewordRKeep the commit, change its message (a message box appears).
EditEStop at this commit so you can change it, then continue.
SquashSCombine with the commit below it, keeping both messages.
FixupFCombine with the commit below it, keeping only that commit's message.
DropDRemove the commit.

Drag rows to reorder them (or use Alt+↑ / Alt+↓). A summary under the list tells you how many commits you will end up with. When you're happy, click Start Rebase.

ExampleFive messy commits become two
  1. Your branch has: Add login form, wip, fix typo, Add login validation, oops, missing test.
  2. Set wip and fix typo to Fixup (they fold into Add login form), and oops, missing test to Fixup (it folds into Add login validation).
  3. Set Add login form to Reword and write a proper message.
  4. Start Rebase. The branch now has two clean commits.
Small change? Skip the editor. A commit's right-click menu has Reword…, Squash into Parent, Fixup into Parent, Drop Commit and Move Up / Move Down. If you commit with fixup! / squash! titles, Autosquash puts them in place for you.

If the rebase stops

  • A conflict: the usual conflict editor opens and shows which step of the rebase you are on. Resolve the files, then Continue — or Skip This Commit, or Abort to go back to where you started.
  • A commit marked Edit: a banner above the graph tells you the rebase is waiting. Change the files, amend the commit, then click Continue (or Abort). The banner comes back if you close the app in the middle.

Undo, and when you need a force-push

  • Undo: the whole rebase — even one that paused for conflicts — is a single step: Edit → Undo or Ctrl+Z.
  • Local changes: if you have uncommitted work, tick Stash my local changes during the rebase; it comes back when the rebase ends.
  • Already pushed? Commits that are already on the remote are marked in the editor. Rewriting them means the remote branch must be replaced: use Push ▾ → Force Push afterwards — and only on a branch nobody else is working on.
Interactive rebase works on the checked-out branch and on ranges without merge commits. It doesn't move commits onto another branch or split a commit inside the editor — mark the commit Edit and split it when the rebase stops there.

From Branch to Merged Pull Request

The whole loop — start from an issue, push, open the pull request, review, merge — without switching to the browser. Works with GitHub pull requests and GitLab merge requests.

Before you start: sign in to GitHub, or add a GitLab token, in Edit → Accounts & SSH Keys… (see Accounts & SSH keys). Public repositories can be browsed without an account; creating, commenting and merging need one. For other hosts, Create Pull Request opens the website's page with your title and description filled in.

Get the repository

  1. Open File → Clone… and switch to your account's tab: your repositories, your organisations' and the ones you starred are listed. Type three letters in the filter to also search the whole host.
  2. Pick HTTPS or SSH, check the destination folder, and click Clone.
  3. Contributing to a project you don't own? Use Fork & Clone: the app forks it into your account, clones your fork and adds the original as upstream. Pull requests then go to the original project.

Start from an issue

  1. Open Repository → Issues: the Issues tab of the bottom panel lists the open issues — Assigned to me, Created by me, Mentioned (GitHub) or All open.
  2. Select the issue and click Start Branch. The name is suggested from the issue — e.g. feature/123-login-times-out, or bugfix/… for issues labelled as bugs — and the branch remembers which issue it is for.
  3. The commit box now shows the issue (#123 Login times out). Click Insert "Fixes #123" in the chip to add the reference to your message.

Push and create the pull request

  1. Commit your work as usual.
  2. Right-click the branch → Create Pull Request…. If the branch isn't pushed yet, the app offers Push and Continue.
  3. Title and description are pre-filled from the branch's commits and the repository's template; for a branch linked to an issue, the description gets Fixes #123 so the issue closes when the pull request merges.
  4. Check the base branch, pick reviewers and labels, tick Create as draft if it isn't ready yet, and create it.

Follow the review and CI

  • The PULL REQUESTS section of the left panel shows each pull request with its review and checks state; right-click it to filter Created by me, Review requested or Drafts.
  • Double-click a pull request to open it in the main view: Conversation, Commits and Files, with diffs in the app's own diff viewer.
  • When your push's CI checks finish, the status bar tells you whether they passed. The selected commit's Checks row lists every check; Re-run Failed restarts the failed jobs.
  • Addressing comments: commit the changes on the same branch and push — the pull request updates by itself. Reply with Comment.

Review someone else's pull request

  1. Open it from the list and read the Files tab.
  2. Want to run it? Check Out brings it to a local branch — also when it comes from a fork. To keep your own work untouched, open that branch in a new worktree instead.
  3. Leave your verdict with Approve, Request Changes or Comment.

Merge

When the pull request is approved and green, click Merge and choose Create a Merge Commit, Squash and Merge or Rebase and Merge, optionally deleting the branch. The merge happens on the server; fetch to see it in the graph.

In-app pull requests, CI status and issues are for GitHub and GitLab. Line-by-line review comments aren't available yet, and issues can be read and created but not edited or closed in the app — Fixes #123 closes them when the change merges.

Review a Pull Request Without Stashing

You're halfway through a change and someone asks you to look at their branch. With a worktree, their branch gets its own folder and tab — your work stays exactly where it is.

Open the branch in a new worktree

  1. Fetch, so the branch is there.
  2. Right-click the branch — in the sidebar or on the graph — → Open in New Worktree.
  3. The branch is checked out in a second folder next to the repository and opens in a new tab, beside the repository's tab and in the same group. The tab title reads repository · folder. A remote branch gets a local branch that tracks it.
  4. Build it, run the tests, read the diffs. Your original tab still has your uncommitted changes, untouched.
Want both side by side? Right-click the new tab → Split Right (see Work on several repositories at once).

More control: New Worktree…

Repository → Worktrees → New Worktree… lets you pick an existing branch or create a new one from any starting point, choose the folder, decide whether to open it in a tab, and initialise its submodules.

“Already checked out”? Switch instead

A branch can be checked out in only one worktree. Branches used elsewhere are marked in the sidebar and in the graph's tooltips, and their Checkout becomes Switch to Its Worktree, which jumps to that tab.

Clean up

  1. In the WORKTREES section of the left panel, right-click the worktree → Remove Worktree…. The app asks first and never silently discards uncommitted changes.
  2. A worktree folder you deleted by hand shows as missing; Prune Missing Worktrees tidies them up.
Worktrees need Git 2.36 or newer. Creating or removing a worktree's folder can't be undone (a new branch created with it can).

Ship a Release with Git-flow

Features, releases and hotfixes the git-flow way — built into the app, no extension to install, and every merge shown to you before it lands.

Set it up once

  1. Repository → Git-flow → Initialise Git-flow…
  2. Pick the production branch (main or master) and the development branch (develop is created if it's missing). Keep the default prefixes (feature/, bugfix/, release/, hotfix/, support/) unless your team uses others, and set the version tag prefix (e.g. v).
  3. Repositories already set up with the git-flow command-line tool are recognised as they are — you can keep using both.

A feature, start to finish

  1. Repository → Git-flow → Start Feature…, type a name (e.g. login-form) → Start. You're on feature/login-form, branched from develop.
  2. Work and commit as usual.
  3. Right-click the branch in the left panel → Finish … (or Repository → Git-flow → Finish). The dialog lists every step: merge into develop, delete the branch, optionally push.
  4. Start Finish. The merge is staged for your review, like any merge in the app; look at it and Commit — the finish carries on by itself.

A release with a version bump

  1. Repository → Git-flow → Start Release…. The version is suggested from the latest version tag — Next Minor or Next Patch — or type your own.
  2. Tick Set this version in pom.xml / package.json to get a “Bump version to …” commit; only the version changes, the file's formatting is kept.
  3. Finish the release: the checklist shows the merge into the production branch, the tag (with its message), the merge back into develop, the branch deletion and the optional push.
  4. Review each staged merge and commit it. Prefer not to? Tick Commit the merges automatically.

Hotfixes work the same way, from the production branch, with the next patch version suggested.

If a finish stops

  • A banner above the graph shows the step you're on. A conflict opens the usual conflict editor; resolve it, commit, and the finish continues.
  • Closed the app in the middle? The finish resumes after a restart — Repository → Git-flow → Continue Finishing ….
  • Abort Finish… stops the plan; steps already done stay done, and Undo reverts them one by one.
The finish doesn't fetch first — fetch before you start; the dialog warns when develop or the production branch are behind. Support branches have no finish, as in the command-line tool.

Get the Verified Badge on GitHub

Sign your commits so GitHub and GitLab show them as Verified. With an SSH key it takes four steps, all on one page.

SSH signing in four steps

  1. Pick a key. Open Edit → Commit Signing…, choose SSH key and select one of your keys — or Create Key… for a new one.
  2. Turn it on. Tick Sign commits (and Sign tags if you want), choose All repositories or This repository only, and Apply.
  3. Upload it. Click Add to GitHub as Signing Key (you need to be signed in to GitHub). For other hosts, Copy Public Key and add it in the website's settings as a signing key.
  4. Test it. Click Test Signing. “Signing works.” means your next commit will be signed.

From then on a lock next to Commit shows that signing is on, and the commit details show who signed each commit. Tick Verify SSH signatures here so your own SSH-signed commits also verify on your machine.

Prefer GPG?

Choose GPG key, pick a key or Create GPG Key (name and email are taken from your Git settings), then upload, apply and test as above. The page warns when your commit email isn't on the key — the usual reason commits show up as Unverified.

Work on Several Repositories at Once

A library and the app that uses it, a front-end and its back-end: put them side by side in one window with tab groups and split view — and add a worktree when you need a second branch of the same one.

Open a group side by side

  1. Put the related repositories in a tab group (right-click a tab → add it to a group).
  2. Right-click the group's chip → Open Side by Side. Its repositories are laid out in panes, up to four.

Or split one tab at a time

  • Right-click a tab → Split Right or Split Down, or drag a tab to the edge of a pane.
  • Drag tabs between panes, or use Move to Pane in a tab's menu.
  • View → Split View has the same commands, plus Join All Panes to go back to one.

What the toolbar acts on

  • The pane you click is the current repository, marked with a coloured border: the toolbar, menus, undo / redo and shortcuts act on it.
  • Move between panes with Ctrl+Alt+← / Ctrl+Alt+→.
  • The other panes keep checking for changes and, with auto fetch, stay up to date. Their progress and messages appear in a slim strip at the top of each pane, so a background job never overwrites what you're looking at.

Layout and restart

Panels are trimmed automatically to fit narrower panes, and the whole layout comes back the next time you start the app. To start over, Join All Panes.

A repository is shown in one pane at a time. To see two branches of the same repository side by side, open one of them in a worktree — it's a separate tab you can split next to the first.
